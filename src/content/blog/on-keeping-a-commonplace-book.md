Before highlighters, before read-later apps, people kept a **commonplace book**: a notebook where they copied out passages worth keeping, along with their own thoughts about them.

Erasmus recommended it. Locke wrote a manual for it. Marcus Aurelius was doing a version of it at night in a tent. It is the most persistent reading-technology in the West, which is worth pausing on, because almost nothing else from the sixteenth century is still in daily use.

## Why copying by hand works

Typing a quote is transcription. Writing a quote by hand is digestion. The slowness forces a decision: *is this actually worth the ink?* Most things are not, and discovering that in the second sentence is the whole benefit.

Then comes the part everyone skips: **your own line underneath.** Not a summary — a reaction. Why did this land? What does it contradict?

## A workable version for this year

- One notebook per year, or one plain text file per year, nothing fancier.
- A page per theme. When you notice the same idea from three different authors, promote it to its own page.
- Re-read it in December with a pen. Mark what still feels true.
- Steal from yourself when you are writing. That is what it is for.

> A commonplace book is a conversation you keep having with the best people you have read, on your own schedule.

Mine is nine years old and mostly boring. It is also the only reason I can find anything I thought before last Tuesday.
