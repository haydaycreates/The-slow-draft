The rule is embarrassingly simple: **nothing arrives before I do.** No inbox, no feeds, no group chats. I sit down with coffee and a blank page, and for forty minutes I am the only person allowed to have a thought.

I have kept this rule for four years and broken it for roughly half of them. Here is what the good half taught me.

## The page is a quieter room

Writing first does not make me more productive. Some mornings I produce three bad paragraphs and delete two. But it puts my attention in one place before the world starts asking for pieces of it, and attention that lands in one place tends to stay there.

> Guard your mornings like a border. Everything that crosses it gets inspected.

There is a version of this advice that turns into a productivity cult — 5 a.m., cold showers, twelve notebooks a year. Ignore that. The point is not the hour. The point is the ordering.

## What actually happens in practice

- Some mornings I outline a post that takes me three more weeks to finish.
- Some mornings I write something that never becomes anything, and that is fine.
- Occasionally I write a sentence I would have paid money for.

**None of those outcomes are predictable in advance.** That is exactly why you have to show up before you can negotiate with yourself about whether today is worth it.

## How to start tomorrow

1. Pick something to write in that opens in under two seconds.
2. Put it somewhere you will trip over it — a physical notebook works unreasonably well.
3. Decide tonight what "done" means for tomorrow: three sentences is a legitimate answer.

That last one matters more than it sounds. A vague intention gets traded away the moment something shiny appears; a tiny, specific one survives.
